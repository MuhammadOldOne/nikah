# Global / Western-diaspora Muslim marriage & matchmaking platforms (state as of Oct 2026): business metrics and product mechanics

Method note (applies to everything below): all findings come from web-search result summaries. Direct page fetching (WebFetch) was blocked in this session for most hosts (e.g. en.wikipedia.org, egyptindependent.com), so primary pages (Companies House PDFs, App Store pages, help-centre pages) could NOT be opened and read in full. Every number below is attributed to the source the search engine surfaced; numbers from affiliate/review blogs and from competitor-owned comparison sites (IslamicKnot, Zawji, Zawjni, NikahForever blog are themselves players in the market) are flagged. Company-claimed user/marriage counts are marketing claims, never audited.

## 1. Who are the major players and what are their business metrics (founding, founders, HQ, funding, users, marriages, revenue, downloads, markets)?

### Takeaway
Muzz (ex-muzmatch, London) is the clear category leader in the West and globally: bootstrapped-then-YC-backed, only ~$8.75M raised in total, ~£24.5M revenue in the year to Jan 2026 (Companies House-derived, reported loss of £1.8M), 17–21M claimed registered users, ~800k claimed marriages, 10M+ Google Play installs. Its only scaled Western rival, Salams (ex-Minder, US), was quietly bought by Match Group (deal closed Oct 2023, disclosed ~early 2025) and is now in a boycott/trust crisis; Match's earlier Muslim brand Hawaya (Egypt) was shut down on 15 Feb 2023 — i.e. the brief's premise that Hawaya is an active Match-backed player is outdated. The rest of the field (SingleMuslim.com, Muslima.com, Pure Matrimony, Half Our Deen, Veil, small newcomers) is a long tail of legacy websites and small apps with self-reported figures only.

### Inferences
- Muzz revenue trajectory from filings: ~£17.8M (FY Jan-2024) → £22.1M (FY Jan-2025, +24%) → £24.5M (FY Jan-2026, ~+11%): growth is decelerating and the company is around break-even/loss-making despite "profitable" claims — likely due to team scaling (76 UK staff on filings vs 150+ global) and India/international expansion.
- Revenue per registered user is low: £24.5M / ~17–21M registered ≈ £1.2–1.4 per registered user per year; with ~10% paying (Wamda 2023), revenue per payer is roughly £15/yr-equivalent on the registered base — implying most registered accounts are dormant and actual paying base is far below 10% of the cumulative registered count. (Estimate, not sourced.)
- Muzz built a ~£24M/yr business on <$9M of venture capital — capital efficiency and organic/word-of-mouth growth, not paid acquisition, define the category leader.
- Match Group has tried three routes into the Muslim niche (buy Muzz — refused; build/buy Hawaya — shut 2023; buy Salams quietly — boycott). This suggests that "Muslim-owned / values-aligned" ownership is itself a competitive moat and a marketing message (Muzz's "Still Muslim-owned" campaign).
- Company-claimed marriage counts (Muzz 800k, Salams 660k "marriages and friendships", Pure Matrimony "500/month", SingleMuslim "100,000") are unverifiable and not comparable; a new entrant should expect such claims to be benchmarked loosely.

## 2. How do the products work (onboarding, profile fields, photo policy, matching, contact initiation, chaperone/wali, verification, moderation, anti-scam)?

### Takeaway
The market has converged on a "Tinder-mechanics + Islamic guardrails" pattern led by Muzz: swipe/like-based discovery with religion filters (sect, prayer frequency, religiosity), mutual-like → free chat, mandatory selfie verification (Muzz via Yoti), optional photo blur for women, and an optional chaperone/wali that receives copies of chats. Differentiators of smaller players are stricter modesty (photos hidden until mutual match — Veil; no photos at all — Zawaj Match; wali-led — Mawadda/Sunnah Match) or a search/browse website model with paid messaging (SingleMuslim, Muslima, Half Our Deen).

### Inferences
- The biggest product-level weaknesses a new nikah site can attack: (1) no verification of marital status/intent, (2) swipe gamification and paid "pay-to-talk" mechanics, (3) optional rather than structural wali involvement, (4) trust in ownership/data (post-Salams).
- Complaints about fakes and unserious users are near-universal in the category and partly structural to free, large-scale platforms; stricter onboarding (ID + wali + intent statements) trades scale for quality.
